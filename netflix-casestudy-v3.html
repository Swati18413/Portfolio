01
The Problem

The Indian
Paradox

Netflix has successfully positioned itself as the world's leading premium streaming platform — driven by powerful original content, a best-in-class recommendation engine, and retention metrics that most competitors can only envy. Globally, Netflix commands loyalty that few subscription businesses achieve. But its story in India tells a different, more complicated tale.

Despite capturing approximately 10% of India's OTT market by value, Netflix accounts for only 6% of total subscribers. In a market with over 272 million OTT users — one of the largest streaming audiences in the world — Netflix is present but not dominant. Competitors like JioHotstar, with 25% market share, and Amazon Prime Video at 23%, have achieved far broader reach through aggressive pricing strategies and years of deep localisation investment. Netflix is winning on revenue per user. It is losing on scale.

This creates a strategic tension that sits at the heart of Netflix's India challenge: the product is perceived as valuable — users who subscribe, pay a premium willingly — but barriers prevent large-scale adoption. Only 24% of India's OTT users pay for content at all, and among those who do pay, Netflix's price point remains 3 to 4 times higher than its most direct competitors. The gap between value share and subscriber share is not a sign of failure. It is, if read correctly, a map of where the opportunity lies.

"Netflix is winning on revenue per user, but losing on scale. The gap between value share and subscriber share is not a weakness — it is an opportunity waiting to be unlocked."

To understand the full shape of the problem, consider the user funnel. India has 272 million OTT users. Of those, only 65 million — roughly 24% — pay for any streaming content. Of those 65 million paying users, 16 million are on Netflix. And of those 16 million, approximately 3% churn every month — a rate above Netflix's own global average of 1 to 2%. At every stage of the funnel, there is a leak. The question is not whether Netflix can grow in India. The question is which leaks to fix first, and in what order.

02
The Audience

Three Indians Netflix
is not fully serving

Netflix's current product model was built around a particular kind of user: someone comfortable paying a premium, consuming English or Hindi content, and watching alone — or at most with one other person. This model works well in metro cities, among young professionals, and among households with high disposable income. It does not work for a large and growing portion of the Indian market.

The first underserved user is the mobile user in Tier 2 and Tier 3 India. This person has a smartphone, a fast data connection, and a genuine appetite for quality content. But they have never owned a television, they access everything through their phone, and they are deeply price-sensitive — not because they don't value entertainment, but because ₹499 per month, for a single screen, feels impossible to justify when free alternatives exist. This user is not rejecting Netflix. They simply haven't been given a way in.

Product implication → For this user, the primary constraint is not interest — it is affordability at scale.

The second underserved user is the regional language speaker — and this is the largest gap of all. India has over 600 million people for whom Hindi is not a primary language. Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Marathi, Kannada, Malayalam — these are not regional niches. They are enormous cultural communities with deep, passionate relationships with their own cinema and storytelling traditions. JioHotstar has spent years building for this audience. Netflix has not. When a Tamil-speaking user opens Netflix and finds limited content in their language, they don't stay. They leave — and they don't come back.

Product implication → For this user, content discovery failure leads to immediate churn.

The third underserved user is the Indian family. The average Indian household is larger, more multi-generational, and more collectively oriented than the Western nuclear household Netflix was originally designed for. Content consumption in India has always been a shared activity — families watching together, parents and children sharing recommendations, friends sharing accounts across cities. Netflix's response to this — cracking down on password sharing — felt punitive in a market where sharing is not a violation of norms but an expression of them. A product that fights culture instead of working with it will always struggle to scale.

Product implication → For this user, value is measured per household, not per individual.
03
Root Cause

Three gaps. One
structural mismatch.

Understanding why Netflix struggles to scale in India requires going deeper than surface observations about price or content. The challenge is structural — a mismatch between a product built for one kind of market and the very different reality of another. At its core, the problem breaks into three fundamental gaps, each of which feeds the others.

The first is the pricing gap. At ₹499 per month for the basic plan, Netflix is three to four times more expensive than JioCinema and Disney+ Hotstar, both of which offer plans starting at ₹149. In a market where only 24% of OTT users pay for content at all, Netflix is asking the audience least accustomed to paying — to pay the most. This is not a positioning problem that can be solved with marketing. It is a structural accessibility problem that requires a product solution.

The second is the content gap. JioHotstar's local content library spans 320,000 hours — roughly six times the size of Netflix India's catalogue. This gap is not just about quantity. It is about relevance. When a user opens a streaming platform and cannot find content that speaks to their language, their culture, their specific regional identity, they experience the product as something built for someone else. That feeling is the fastest route to churn. Content is not a feature in streaming. It is the product itself.

The third is the cultural gap. Netflix's password sharing crackdown, however logical from a global revenue perspective, landed poorly in India — a market where household structures are multi-generational, consumption is collective, and sharing is a deeply ingrained social behaviour. A product decision that made sense globally created genuine friction locally. This is what happens when global strategy doesn't account for local culture — users don't just disengage, they feel disrespected. And in a market built on word of mouth and community recommendation, that feeling travels fast.

04
What Netflix Built Right

The foundations that made
Netflix unbeatable

To build a credible strategy for Netflix India, it helps to first understand what made Netflix structurally dominant everywhere else — because the foundations are real, and they matter.

In 2007, Netflix made the shift from DVD rentals to streaming. Almost immediately, internet infrastructure became the product's biggest vulnerability. Millions of users watching simultaneously created enormous strain on delivery networks, and buffering — slow loading, blurry video, interrupted playback — was destroying the experience. Netflix initially relied on third-party content delivery networks, as most companies did. But third-party infrastructure meant third-party limitations. So in 2012, Netflix made a decision that would define the company: they built their own CDN from scratch, called Open Connect.

Instead of routing video from distant data centres, Open Connect placed Netflix's own servers — called Open Connect Appliances — inside internet service providers around the world. Popular content was stored close to where users lived. When someone pressed play, the video came from nearby, not from across the world. The result was faster load times, fewer interruptions, lower costs, and — crucially — complete control over the experience. This single infrastructure decision is why Netflix streams more reliably than almost any competitor. The lesson it teaches is simple but powerful: when something is core to your user experience, you build it yourself even if it's hard.

The second foundational decision was original content. In 2013, Netflix launched House of Cards — its first major original production. The show was a hit, but more importantly, it proved something: Netflix could compete with Hollywood, not just distribute its output. What followed — Stranger Things, The Crown, Narcos, and then an ambitious global strategy that produced Squid Game from South Korea, Money Heist from Spain, and Delhi Crime from India — was the most aggressive original content investment in television history. Owning content gave Netflix control over what stayed on the platform, made its catalogue exclusive and therefore irreplaceable, and allowed data to drive creative decisions in ways that no traditional studio could replicate.

These two decisions — owning infrastructure and owning content — created a product that was genuinely hard to replicate. The challenge in India is that neither advantage is sufficient when the price excludes most of the market and the content doesn't speak most users' languages.

04b
Technical Foundation

How Netflix broke itself
to become unbreakable

Beyond content and infrastructure, there is a third thing Netflix built that no competitor has replicated at the same depth: a culture of deliberate failure. In 2011, while migrating to a cloud-based microservices architecture on AWS, Netflix engineers faced a fundamental problem. A distributed system with hundreds of interdependent microservices means hundreds of potential failure points — and the only way to know how the system behaves when one breaks is to break it on purpose.

"The best way to avoid failure is to fail constantly." — Netflix Engineering, 2011

The answer was Chaos Engineering — and Netflix's implementation of it, through a suite of tools called the Simian Army, is one of the most consequential technical decisions in streaming history. Chaos Monkey, the original tool, randomly terminated virtual machine instances in Netflix's production environment — during business hours, on live traffic. The logic was counterintuitive but airtight: if your system can survive random failures in controlled conditions, you can be confident it will survive unpredictable failures in the real world. Engineers who know their services will be randomly killed are engineers who build redundancy and fault tolerance by default.

🐒 Chaos Monkey
Instance Terminator
Randomly shuts down individual server instances during business hours. Forces engineers to build redundancy into every microservice from day one.
🦍 Chaos Gorilla
Availability Zone Killer
Simulates the failure of an entire Amazon Availability Zone — testing whether the system routes traffic and recovers without user-visible disruption.
🦁 Chaos Kong
Region-Level Failure
Drops an entire AWS region. The most extreme test — ensures Netflix can survive a catastrophic regional outage with minimal downtime globally.
⏱️ Latency Monkey
Network Degradation
Injects artificial network latency between services. Tests how gracefully features degrade — important for India's variable mobile network conditions.

The results are extraordinary. Netflix experiences only a few minutes of downtime per year — despite running one of the most complex distributed systems ever built, serving 325 million subscribers across 190 countries simultaneously. When a real AWS region went down in 2014 and rebooted 10% of servers, Netflix handled it without significant user impact — because they had already rehearsed exactly that scenario through Chaos Engineering.

PM Lens — What This Means for India
Chaos Engineering is not just a technical strategy — it is a product reliability strategy. In India, where mobile connectivity is variable and Tier 2 users often face bandwidth constraints, a platform that degrades gracefully under network stress is a product that retains users. A buffering screen is a churn event. Netflix's chaos engineering infrastructure means that even when backend services degrade, the experience adapts — lower resolution, cached content, pre-fetched recommendations — rather than failing hard. For the ₹149 mobile user I am proposing, this reliability is not a nice-to-have. It is the product promise.
05
The Strategy

What I would do as
PM at Netflix India

The objective is clear and measurable: grow Netflix India's subscriber base from 16 million to 25 million users within 24 months, while maintaining sustainable revenue growth. This translates into increasing subscriber share from approximately 6% to 9%, expanding meaningfully beyond metro and premium segments, and improving retention across diverse user groups. The strategy to get there focuses on three core levers: Accessibility, Relevance, and Household Fit. Each initiative maps directly to one of the three root cause gaps.

Initiative 01 · Accessibility
₹149 Mobile-Only Ad-Supported Plan

The most direct way to close the accessibility gap is to create a product tier that meets the mobile-first, price-sensitive Tier 2 and Tier 3 user where they actually are — without devaluing the core Netflix experience. I would introduce a ₹149 mobile-only, ad-supported plan that offers the complete Netflix content library on a single mobile screen, with a limited number of ads per hour. The content is identical. The experience is smaller. The price is competitive.

This is not a new idea for Netflix — they have deployed similar tiers successfully in other emerging markets including Indonesia and Kenya. The key for India is positioning. This plan should not be framed as the "cheap Netflix." It should be framed as "Netflix for your phone" — a different form factor for a different context, with the same quality of content that makes Netflix worth paying for in the first place.

Initiative 02 · Relevance
"Made for India" Regional Content Programme

Content is the primary driver of retention in streaming. A user who finds something they love on a platform stays. A user who opens the app and finds nothing that speaks to them leaves — and they rarely come back. JioHotstar has spent years building a local content library that now spans 320,000 hours, approximately six times the size of Netflix India's catalogue. This is not a gap that can be closed overnight. But it can be closed strategically.

I would launch a "Made for India" content programme — a formal commitment to produce 50 original Indian titles per year across 8 regional languages: Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Marathi, Kannada, Malayalam, Punjabi, and Gujarati. Not just Hindi. Real regional. The programme would be structured as a flywheel: more regional content drives higher engagement among non-Hindi speakers, higher engagement reduces churn, lower churn increases lifetime value, and higher lifetime value funds more regional content investment.

India has over 600 million people for whom Hindi is not the primary language. These users are not a niche — they are the majority of the market Netflix is not yet serving. Regional language content is not a concession to local preferences. It is the single most direct path to the next 5 million Netflix subscribers.

Initiative 03 · Household Fit
₹699 Family Plan — Redesigned for Indian Homes

The password sharing crackdown was a globally rational decision that landed as a locally tone-deaf one. In India, shared consumption is not a loophole — it is how families and friend groups have always experienced entertainment together. Responding to this with restrictions created friction where Netflix needed trust. The fix is not to enforce harder. The fix is to build a product that acknowledges and serves how Indian households actually work.

I would introduce a ₹699 Family Plan supporting up to 6 screens simultaneously, with fully personalised profiles per family member, regional language preferences per profile, and robust parental controls. Critically, this plan would be positioned not as "we are allowing sharing" but as "Netflix was made for your whole family." The framing transforms what was a perceived punishment into a genuine value proposition — and at ₹699 for six screens, it is one of the most competitive per-person prices in the Indian streaming market.

Product Innovation

Six features I would build
as Brand + Product lead

Beyond the three strategic initiatives, there are several product and brand decisions I would make at Netflix India — rooted in what I see as a real user and what I understand about the Indian consumer relationship with content and technology.

The first is Favourites Collections — a feature that lets users build and share personal collections of the movies and shows they love. Think of it as a mood board for your cinematic identity. A group of college friends could create a shared "College Days" collection, adding every film and show that defined their time together — Taare Zameen Par, Dil Chahta Hai, Friends, How I Met Your Mother. A family could build a Sunday classics list. Collections would be shareable directly within Netflix, creating a social layer that deepens emotional connection to the platform far beyond what any algorithm can manufacture. The best recommendation a user can receive is from someone they trust. This feature makes Netflix the place where those recommendations live.

ProblemAlgorithmic discovery lacks social trust — users want human recommendations, not just data-driven ones
Why NetflixNetflix's breadth of catalogue makes a curation layer more valuable here than on any niche platform
Metric+25% increase in content completion rate for shared collection titles

The second is a mood-adaptive UI powered by AI. Netflix already knows what you love — its recommendation engine is built on that knowledge. But the product doesn't yet reflect that knowledge in the way it looks and feels. If a user is deep into Bridgerton, opening Netflix should feel, subtly, like entering that world — warm tones, period-adjacent aesthetic details, an interface that whispers "we know what you love" without ever saying it out loud. Netflix's iconic red-and-black identity would remain dominant. The chrome, the logo, the navigation — all Netflix. But the emotional temperature of the experience would shift to reflect the user's world.

The average Indian urban professional spends 45 to 90 minutes commuting every day. That is an enormous content consumption window that no Indian streaming platform has seriously built for. Commute Mode would curate short content automatically when a user is on the move — optimised for mobile screens, offline-capable, and designed to fit perfectly into the gaps of a busy day.

The fourth is a weekly genre check-in. At the start of each week, Netflix asks: "Hey, what's the plan — comedy, romance, thriller, documentary?" The entire homepage reorganises around the answer. This solves one of Netflix's most consistently reported frustrations: decision fatigue. Users spend more time choosing what to watch than actually watching. A single weekly signal — gathered in one tap — gives the recommendation engine far richer context and gives users a homepage that feels curated for that specific week in their life.

The fifth is a simple but important navigation fix. The exit button on Netflix currently requires too many steps to reach — users have to scroll through multiple layers of menus to find it. It should be permanently accessible in the left panel on every screen. This is a small change but it signals something that matters: respect for the user's time. The best products don't make exits difficult. They make the experience good enough that users choose to stay — and that is a very different thing.

The sixth addresses an audience that is growing rapidly and almost entirely ignored: Indian users over 50. Netflix's core demographic is 18 to 39 — an audience for whom navigating thousands of titles is intuitive and even enjoyable. But India's over-50 population is large, has significant disposable income, and has a deep emotional relationship with regional cinema, classic television, and local storytelling. A dedicated "Regional Mode" with simplified navigation, larger text, and a curated catalogue of local language content would unlock this audience — and, crucially, would make Netflix the platform that the entire Indian household uses, not just its youngest members.

Execution

24 months to
25 million subscribers

Strategy without execution is fiction. The three initiatives and six product innovations above are sequenced into a 24-month roadmap designed to generate early momentum, build on each phase's learnings, and reach the 25 million subscriber target without requiring a fundamental overhaul of what makes Netflix great.

Prioritisation Logic

The roadmap prioritises reducing entry barriers first, followed by increasing relevance, and finally optimising retention. This sequencing is intentional — there is no point improving retention before you have users, and no point improving relevance before users can afford to access the product. Each phase builds the foundation for the next.

08
PM Lessons

Three things Netflix taught me
about great products

The most valuable thing about studying Netflix is not the specific decisions it made. It is the way it thinks about products — as living systems that must constantly adapt, not as finished objects to be launched and maintained.

The first lesson is that your product is never finished. Netflix moved from DVDs to streaming, from licensing to originals, from originals to global originals, from global to local. At each stage, a previous version of the company would have seen the next transition as too risky, too expensive, or too uncertain. Instead, Netflix treated each version of itself as a foundation for the next. Many product teams treat launch as the finish line. Netflix treats it as a starting point. The market will change, user behaviour will evolve, and technology will create new possibilities. The only safe strategy is to move before you are forced to.

The second lesson is to build the core yourself when it matters. Netflix could have continued relying on third-party CDNs when streaming grew. Most companies would have. Instead, they invested years and significant capital into building Open Connect — because they understood that streaming quality was not a technical detail, it was the product. When your core user experience depends on infrastructure you don't control, your product becomes fragile in ways that are very difficult to fix. The question every PM should ask is: what is the one thing our product cannot afford to get wrong? Whatever the answer is — build it yourself.

The third lesson is that data gives direction, but testing tells the truth. Netflix runs A/B tests at massive scale — on thumbnails, on autoplay settings, on episode release schedules, on UI layouts. They do not just collect data and act on what it suggests. They form hypotheses, build experiments, and let reality tell them whether they were right. This culture of validation rather than assumption is what allows Netflix to move fast without making expensive mistakes. The lesson for any PM is this: use data to inform your roadmap, but do not confuse data analysis with knowing. Test quickly. Learn fast. Kill what doesn't work and invest more in what does.

"If I were building Netflix India, I would prioritise speed of learning over perfection — because in a market like India, the biggest risk is not failure. It is moving too slowly."
